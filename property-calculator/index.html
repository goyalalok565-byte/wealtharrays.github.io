What this calculator does

Estimate upfront cash, EMI and total home-buying cost from price, down payment, financing and transaction costs. It keeps the assumptions visible so you can change one input at a time and understand what drives the result.

How the calculation works

Upfront cash = down payment + stamp duty + registration + other upfront costs.

Inputs

The calculator uses property price, down payment, stamp duty, registration, loan rate, term and other upfront costs. Change the assumptions and click Calculate result to compare scenarios.

Limitations and methodology

Wealth Arrays performs deterministic browser-side calculations. It does not automatically know current market rates, lender policy, tax law, fees or personal eligibility. Verify current figures with the relevant official source or provider before making a material decision.

Frequently asked questions

Why is EMI not the full cost?

A purchase also needs a down payment, transaction charges and potentially maintenance, insurance and taxes.

Does this include appreciation?

No. Property appreciation is uncertain.

Can I compare renting with this?

Yes, but a fair comparison needs rent growth, investment returns, maintenance, taxes and opportunity cost.

Property purchase planning: compare price with ownership cost

A property decision includes more than the quoted purchase price. Financing, transaction taxes, maintenance, repairs, insurance, vacancy risk for rentals and eventual sale costs can change the economics. The right inputs depend on whether you are buying to live in the property, renting it out or comparing it with renting. Use this calculator as a scenario framework and keep one-time acquisition costs separate from recurring ownership expenses.

Compare like with like

For two properties, use the same holding period and include comparable costs: stamp duty, registration, repairs, annual maintenance, property tax, insurance and likely selling expenses. If you are evaluating rental income, use realistic occupancy, maintenance and tax assumptions rather than gross rent alone. Property appreciation is uncertain and should be stress-tested instead of treated as guaranteed.

Separate personal value from investment return

A home can provide stability and utility that a simple investment return does not capture. A rental property also concentrates money in one asset and may be difficult to sell quickly. Compare the cash required, monthly affordability, liquidity and downside scenarios. Confirm legal title, approvals, encumbrances and the exact cost sheet before committing.

Separate rental yield, ownership costs and appreciation

For an investment property, distinguish gross rental yield from net cash flow after maintenance, vacancy, repairs, insurance, property tax, management costs and financing. For an owner-occupied home, the benefit includes housing utility but should still be compared with ongoing costs and alternative uses of the deposit. Property appreciation is uncertain and a sale can involve brokerage, legal fees and taxes. Use the same holding period when comparing properties and include realistic assumptions for downtime and repairs. Before buying, verify title, approvals, encumbrances, possession terms and the full cost sheet. A calculator can organise scenarios, but it cannot inspect the property, validate legal documents or guarantee resale value.

Test downside scenarios before buying

Run a conservative scenario with lower rent, a longer vacancy, higher maintenance and little or no property appreciation. For a financed purchase, test higher interest costs and include the full down payment plus transaction expenses in the initial cash requirement. For an owner-occupied home, compare the ownership costs with a realistic rent alternative over the same period, while recognising that lifestyle and stability also matter. Selling can take time and incur fees, so avoid assuming the property can be converted to cash immediately. Verify legal title, approvals and the property's physical condition independently; a calculator cannot inspect these risks.

Worked example with Indian rupees

Suppose a property costs ₹60,00,000 and one-time costs add an illustrative 7% (₹4,20,000). The acquisition cash requirement is then ₹64,20,000 before interiors, financing fees or repairs. If you borrow part of the amount, add total loan interest and ongoing maintenance to the ownership scenario. The 7% is an assumption, not a standard charge; replace it with written local quotes.
