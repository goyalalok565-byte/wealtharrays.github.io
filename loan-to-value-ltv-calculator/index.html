Loan-to-value ratio: understand the collateral share

Loan-to-value (LTV) compares the loan amount with the value accepted by the lender for the property or asset. It is usually expressed as a percentage: loan amount ÷ eligible asset value × 100. A higher LTV means the borrower finances a larger share of the value and may have less equity. LTV is only one part of underwriting; lenders also assess income, credit, title, property type and applicable regulatory or product limits.

Use the lender's eligible valuation

The purchase price and the lender's accepted valuation may not be identical. Some fees, land components or property types may be treated differently under a product's rules. If you use an inflated or outdated market estimate, the resulting LTV may be misleading. Confirm the valuation method and whether the calculation includes only the loan principal or other financed charges.

LTV is not the same as affordability

A low LTV does not prove that the monthly instalment is affordable, and a high LTV does not by itself establish rejection. Consider down payment, EMI, existing debt, income stability and transaction costs together. Check current lender terms and any applicable regulatory ceilings rather than treating a generic percentage as universal.

LTV should be read alongside cash contribution

LTV helps show how much of the eligible asset value is financed by the loan, but it does not measure whether the monthly repayment fits the borrower's budget. The cash contribution may need to cover the difference between the lender's accepted valuation and the loan, plus stamp duty, registration and other costs that are not financed. If the valuation is lower than the purchase price, the required down payment can rise. Check whether fees are included in the loan amount for the calculation and use the lender's accepted valuation rather than a listing price. Compare LTV with EMI affordability, income stability and existing debt before making a purchase commitment.

Estimate the cash gap before purchase

If the lender accepts a value below the seller's price, the borrower may need to cover that difference from personal funds in addition to the normal down payment and transaction costs. For example, an LTV calculated on a lender valuation does not automatically tell you how much cash is needed to close if the agreement price is higher. Confirm which valuation is used and whether any financed fee is included in the loan principal. Keep a separate estimate for stamp duty, registration, legal costs and repairs. A low LTV can reduce leverage but does not replace an affordability assessment or a legal review of the collateral.

Worked example with Indian rupees

If the requested loan is ₹40,00,000 and the lender accepts the property value as ₹50,00,000, LTV is 80% (₹40 lakh ÷ ₹50 lakh × 100). If the lender's valuation is ₹45,00,000 instead, the same loan would imply an LTV of about 88.9%. That difference may affect the amount a lender is willing to approve, but the applicable cap depends on the product and current rules.
