What this calculator does

Find the break-even units and sales value from fixed costs, selling price and variable cost, plus units needed for a profit target. Change the assumptions to compare outcomes. The result is an estimate based on the inputs you provide, not a promise or personalised financial recommendation.

How the calculation works

Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit. Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit, rounded up to a whole unit. Break-even sales = break-even units × selling price. Units for a profit target = (fixed costs + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit. If the selling price does not exceed the variable cost, no sales volume can cover the fixed costs.

How to use this calculator

For this calculator, check these inputs and units: Fixed costs per month; Selling price per unit; Variable cost per unit; Target monthly profit; Days open per month; Contribution per unit; Break-even sales per month; Break-even units per day; Units per month for the profit target. Keep monthly and annual figures separate, and change one input at a time when comparing scenarios.

Read the result in context

The output is the mathematical result of the assumptions entered, not a forecast of what a bank, employer, tax authority or investment provider will necessarily apply. Separate amounts you contribute or pay from interest, growth, tax or other calculated components where the tool provides them. A larger final amount is not always a better outcome: total interest, liquidity, affordability, risk and the time available can matter just as much as the headline number. Keep a note of your inputs when comparing two scenarios.

Compare more than one scenario

Run a baseline estimate first. Then create a conservative case and a second case with one important assumption changed, such as the rate, period, payment or contribution. Comparing scenarios is more informative than selecting a single result because it shows how sensitive the outcome is to the inputs. For long periods, even a small change in an assumed rate can have a large effect. For short periods, fees, timing and fixed charges can be relatively more important than a percentage-rate difference.

What this estimate does not include

This calculator does not automatically know current product terms, policy changes, eligibility conditions, fees, taxes, penalties, contribution ceilings or personal circumstances. Those items can change the real amount you receive or pay. For tax-related estimates, verify the applicable tax year, regime and official rules. For deposits, loans and retirement products, compare the result with the provider current documentation. If the result will affect a major financial decision, confirm the assumptions with a qualified professional or the relevant official source.

Example inputs

One illustrative set of inputs is: Fixed costs per month: 150000; Selling price per unit: 150; Variable cost per unit: 60; Target monthly profit: 50000; Days open per month: 30. Use your own figures for a more relevant estimate.

Frequently asked questions

What are fixed and variable costs?

Fixed costs such as rent, salaries and licences stay the same however much you sell in a month. Variable costs such as ingredients and packaging rise with every unit sold.

Why does the answer say there is no break-even?

That happens when the selling price is equal to or below the variable cost. Each sale then loses money or adds nothing, so more sales never cover the fixed costs. Raise the price or cut the variable cost.

Can I use it for a service business?

Yes. Treat one billable hour, one job or one order as a unit, use the average price per unit, and enter the direct cost of delivering that unit as the variable cost.

Important assumptions and limitations

This tool models the formula and assumptions described above. Actual outcomes can differ because of fees, taxes, eligibility rules, contribution timing, rate changes and individual circumstances. Check current official guidance or your provider's terms before making a financial decision.

What a break-even number tells a small business

Break-even is the volume at which your sales just cover your costs, so the business neither loses nor makes money. It depends on the contribution per unit, which is the selling price minus the variable cost of one unit. Every sale contributes that amount towards the fixed costs, and once the fixed costs are covered the contribution becomes profit. The calculator rounds up to whole units because you cannot sell part of a unit. It also divides by the days you are open so that a monthly target becomes a daily one that a small team can track. If the price does not exceed the variable cost, the page says there is no break-even.

Price and variable cost move the answer fastest

A small price increase raises the contribution on every unit and can cut the break-even volume by a surprising amount, as the ₹170 example shows. A small cut in the cost of ingredients or packaging has the same effect. Test price changes against how many customers you might lose, because a higher price only helps if demand holds up.

Keep the cost categories clean

Put rent, salaries and licences in fixed costs, and put only the cost that rises with each sale in variable costs. Mixing the two can make the result misleading. If you sell several products, use an average price and an average variable cost weighted by your usual sales mix, and update the figures whenever your supplier prices or rent change.

Worked example with Indian rupees

With fixed costs of ₹1,50,000 a month, a selling price of ₹150 and a variable cost of ₹60, the contribution is ₹90 per unit. Break-even is 1,667 units a month, or sales of about ₹2,50,050, which is about 56 units a day over 30 days. To also earn a ₹50,000 monthly profit you need 2,223 units. Raising the price to ₹170 lowers break-even to 1,364 units, and cutting fixed costs to ₹1,20,000 lowers it to 1,334 units.
