What this calculator does

Compare buying a home on a loan with renting and investing, using price, loan terms, rent, appreciation and investment return. Change the assumptions to compare outcomes. The result is an estimate based on the inputs you provide, not a promise or personalised financial recommendation.

How the calculation works

Buying: down payment plus buying costs are paid upfront, the loan is repaid at a fixed EMI, and a yearly maintenance and property-tax cost is added. The home value grows at the assumed appreciation rate and the outstanding loan is deducted. Renting: the same upfront cash is invested, rent rises every year, and whichever option has the lower monthly outflow invests the difference at the assumed return. The result compares net worth at the end of the chosen period. Selling costs, tax benefits and capital-gains tax are not modelled.

How to use this calculator

For this calculator, check these inputs and units: Home price; Down payment; Home-loan interest rate; Loan term; Expected yearly home appreciation; Monthly rent for a similar home; Yearly rent increase; Expected annual return on invested money; Yearly maintenance and property tax; Stamp duty, registration and other buying costs; How long you plan to stay; Monthly loan EMI; Net worth if you buy; Net worth if you rent and invest. Keep monthly and annual figures separate, and change one input at a time when comparing scenarios.

Read the result in context

The output is the mathematical result of the assumptions entered, not a forecast of what a bank, employer, tax authority or investment provider will necessarily apply. Separate amounts you contribute or pay from interest, growth, tax or other calculated components where the tool provides them. A larger final amount is not always a better outcome: total interest, liquidity, affordability, risk and the time available can matter just as much as the headline number. Keep a note of your inputs when comparing two scenarios.

Compare more than one scenario

Run a baseline estimate first. Then create a conservative case and a second case with one important assumption changed, such as the rate, period, payment or contribution. Comparing scenarios is more informative than selecting a single result because it shows how sensitive the outcome is to the inputs. For long periods, even a small change in an assumed rate can have a large effect. For short periods, fees, timing and fixed charges can be relatively more important than a percentage-rate difference.

What this estimate does not include

This calculator does not automatically know current product terms, policy changes, eligibility conditions, fees, taxes, penalties, contribution ceilings or personal circumstances. Those items can change the real amount you receive or pay. For tax-related estimates, verify the applicable tax year, regime and official rules. For deposits, loans and retirement products, compare the result with the provider current documentation. If the result will affect a major financial decision, confirm the assumptions with a qualified professional or the relevant official source.

Example inputs

Use your own figures for a more relevant estimate.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying always better than renting?

No. The answer depends on the price-to-rent ratio, the loan rate, how fast the home appreciates and what the invested money earns. This calculator lets you test those assumptions side by side.

Why is the investment return so important?

Renting only wins if the money you do not spend on a down payment and EMI is actually invested and earns a return. A lower return makes buying look better and a higher return makes renting look better.

Does it include home-loan tax benefits?

No. Tax deductions on interest and principal depend on the regime you choose and change over time, so they are left out. Add them mentally as a small improvement to the buying result.

Important assumptions and limitations

This tool models the formula and assumptions described above. Actual outcomes can differ because of fees, taxes, eligibility rules, contribution timing, rate changes and individual circumstances. Check current official guidance or your provider's terms before making a financial decision.

How to compare renting and buying on equal terms

A fair rent-versus-buy comparison gives both choices the same starting cash and the same monthly budget. Here the buyer pays the down payment, the buying costs, the EMI and a yearly upkeep cost. The renter invests the same upfront cash, pays rent that rises every year, and invests whatever monthly amount the buyer would have spent beyond the rent. Net worth at the end is home value minus the outstanding loan for the buyer and the investment portfolio for the renter. The comparison is most sensitive to the price-to-rent ratio, the loan rate and the return you can realistically earn on invested money, so test those three first.

What the result leaves out

Selling costs, capital-gains tax, home-loan tax deductions, the value of living in a home you own and the emotional value of stability are not modelled. Some of these favour buying, such as tax deductions and the protection from future rent increases. Others favour renting, such as flexibility and lower transaction costs. Use the number as one input, not as the final answer.

Run the break-even test on rent and holding period

Increase the monthly rent until the two net worth figures come close, and note the price-to-rent ratio at that point. Then lengthen the stay. Buying costs such as stamp duty are paid once, so a longer stay spreads them over more years and usually helps the buyer. A short stay of a few years rarely favours buying because the upfront costs have little time to be recovered.

Worked example with Indian rupees

For an ₹80,00,000 home with a 20% down payment, an 8.75% loan over 20 years, 5% yearly appreciation, ₹25,000 monthly rent rising 5% a year, a 10% investment return, 1% yearly upkeep, 6% buying costs and a 10-year stay, the EMI is about ₹56,557. Buying leaves a net worth of about ₹85.2 lakh and renting while investing leaves about ₹1.24 crore, so renting is ahead by about ₹39.3 lakh. Lowering the investment return to 7% narrows the gap to about ₹13.5 lakh, and a ₹40,000 rent narrows it to about ₹1.8 lakh.
