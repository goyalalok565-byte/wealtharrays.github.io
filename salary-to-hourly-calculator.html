What this calculator does

The salary-to-hourly calculator converts an annual salary into an hourly equivalent using the work schedule assumptions you provide. It is useful for comparing salaried compensation with hourly rates, contract offers or changes in working hours.

The result is an equivalent rate, not necessarily the amount that will appear on a payslip. Taxes, benefits, unpaid leave, overtime rules and employer-specific compensation can make actual take-home pay different.

How the calculation works

The basic calculation divides annual pay by the estimated number of paid working hours in a year. That means the result depends on both salary and schedule. If you assume 40 hours per week and 52 paid weeks, the annual hour count is 2,080. If you allow unpaid weeks, holidays or a different weekly schedule, the equivalent hourly rate changes.

There is no single universally correct number of annual working hours. The most useful result is the one based on a schedule that matches the situation you are comparing.

Worked example

Suppose annual salary is $62,400 and the schedule is 40 hours per week for 52 paid weeks. The simple equivalent is $62,400 ÷ 2,080 = $30 per hour before taxes and other deductions. If four weeks are unpaid, the same salary is earned over fewer paid hours and the equivalent hourly rate is higher.

How to use the result responsibly

When comparing a job offer, include benefits and unpaid time where relevant. Health insurance, retirement contributions, bonuses, paid leave, commuting time and other benefits can materially change the economic value of an offer. A salary-to-hourly conversion is only one part of that comparison.

Overtime is also jurisdiction- and employer-specific. Do not assume that an equivalent hourly rate is the legal overtime rate.

Limitations and methodology

Wealth Arrays performs the arithmetic from your inputs in the browser. It does not know your employment contract, tax jurisdiction or benefit package. The calculator therefore presents a transparent equivalent rather than a payroll or legal calculation.

Frequently asked questions

Why can 48 weeks be useful instead of 52?

If you expect unpaid leave or non-working weeks, using fewer paid weeks can produce a more realistic equivalent hourly rate. The right assumption depends on the job.

Does this include overtime?

Not automatically. Overtime rules vary by jurisdiction and contract. Use the overtime calculator separately when its assumptions match your situation.

Are taxes included?

No. The result is a gross compensation equivalent unless you deliberately enter a net-pay scenario using another method.

Can freelancers use this?

Yes as a reference point, but freelancers should also account for non-billable time, benefits, business expenses, taxes and gaps between projects. The freelance-rate calculator can help with that broader calculation.

Think about total work time

If a salaried role requires a long commute, frequent unpaid overtime or substantial after-hours work, the practical hourly value can be lower than a simple paid-hours calculation suggests. For contract work, non-billable administration and time spent finding projects can have the opposite effect. Include the assumptions that matter to the comparison you are actually making.
