What this calculator does

Calculate interest and total repayment on a standard fixed-rate amortizing loan. It keeps the assumptions visible so you can change one input at a time and understand what drives the result.

How the calculation works

Total interest = scheduled repayments − original principal.

Inputs

The calculator uses loan principal, annual interest rate and loan term. Change the assumptions and click Calculate result to compare scenarios.

Limitations and methodology

Wealth Arrays performs deterministic browser-side calculations. It does not automatically know current market rates, lender policy, tax law, fees or personal eligibility. Verify current figures with the relevant official source or provider before making a material decision.

Frequently asked questions

Why can total interest be so high?

Long terms create many interest-bearing months, especially at higher rates.

Does this match every lender?

It assumes standard monthly amortization with a constant rate.

How can I reduce interest?

A lower rate, shorter term or extra principal payments can reduce it, subject to lender rules.

Loan interest: distinguish interest cost from total repayment

Loan interest depends on the outstanding balance, rate convention, payment timing and how the lender applies each payment. A reducing-balance loan charges interest on the amount still owed, so early payments typically contain a larger interest share. A flat-rate quote can look smaller but may produce a higher effective cost. This calculator provides an estimate from the inputs shown; compare the result with the lender's amortisation schedule and fee disclosure.

Reducing balance versus flat rate

With a reducing-balance method, interest is calculated on the remaining principal each period. With a flat-rate quote, interest may be calculated on the original principal even while instalments repay it. The same headline percentage can therefore describe different costs. Ask the lender for APR/effective rate, total amount payable and a payment schedule before comparing products.

What may sit outside the interest result

Processing fees, documentation charges, compulsory insurance, late fees and prepayment penalties may not be included in an interest-only estimate. For floating-rate loans, future rates may change the interest path. Keep principal, interest and fees as separate lines in your comparison so that the total borrowing cost is visible.

Check the lender's method and all-in cost

Request the lender's amortisation schedule so you can see how principal and interest change over time. Confirm whether the advertised rate is flat, reducing-balance, fixed or floating, and whether it is nominal or an effective annual rate. Add processing fees, documentation costs, compulsory insurance and prepayment penalties to the comparison where relevant. For a floating-rate loan, the future interest total can change when the rate resets; a fixed-rate example should not be treated as a forecast for that product. Keep interest cost separate from principal repayment and other fees. The calculator is an estimate based on the inputs provided, so use the contract and payment schedule to confirm the final borrowing cost.

Check whether extra payments change the schedule

If you plan to make extra repayments, ask whether the lender applies them immediately to principal and whether the monthly EMI or remaining term changes. Some products have minimum prepayment amounts, notice requirements or charges. For a floating-rate loan, the interest total can move when the benchmark changes; the current schedule is not a promise of future interest. Compare the contract's total amount payable with the calculator and include fees separately. If the lender uses a flat-rate method, do not compare its nominal percentage directly with a reducing-balance rate without converting the effective cost. These checks help prevent a seemingly low rate from hiding a more expensive loan.

Worked example with Indian rupees

For a simple interest-only illustration, ₹5,00,000 at 10% for one year produces ₹50,000 of interest if the principal remains outstanding for the full year. A reducing-balance EMI loan will usually have a different interest total because the principal falls with each payment. Do not use a simple-interest multiplication as the total cost of an amortising loan unless the contract actually uses that method.
