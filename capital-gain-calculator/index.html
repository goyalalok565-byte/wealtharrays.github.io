What this calculator does

Estimate a simple capital gain from purchase and sale values after eligible expenses, using a tax rate you enter. It keeps the assumptions visible so you can change one input at a time and understand what drives the result.

How the calculation works

Capital gain = sale proceeds − purchase cost − eligible expenses.

Inputs

The calculator uses Purchase price / cost basis, Sale proceeds, Purchase-related eligible costs, Selling costs, Assumed tax rate. Change the assumptions and click Calculate result to compare scenarios.

Limitations and methodology

Wealth Arrays performs deterministic browser-side calculations. It does not automatically know current market rates, lender policy, tax law, fees or personal eligibility. Verify current figures with the relevant official source or provider before making a material financial decision.

Capital gains: distinguish sale proceeds, cost and tax

A capital-gain estimate starts with sale consideration and the relevant cost basis, then adjusts for eligible expenses or reliefs only where the applicable rules allow them. The resulting gain is not automatically the taxable amount or final tax payable. Holding period, asset class, acquisition date, residency, exemptions, indexation rules and the tax year can materially change treatment. This calculator is an arithmetic aid; it does not determine the correct legal classification or apply every current tax provision.

Gain is not the same as tax liability

A positive gain is only one step in a tax calculation. Some assets have special holding-period rules or tax rates; some transactions may qualify for exemptions or require adjustments. Loss set-off and reporting requirements can also matter. Use the result as a preliminary reconciliation, then check the official Income Tax Department guidance for the relevant financial year or consult a qualified tax professional for a filing decision.

Keep records that support the cost basis

Useful records may include contract notes, purchase invoices, broker statements, transfer fees and evidence of improvement costs where relevant. Avoid double-counting costs or including an expense that the applicable rules do not permit. If the asset was acquired through inheritance, gifting, corporate action or multiple purchases, the cost basis may require additional analysis. A single input cannot resolve those legal details.

Evidence to gather before using a tax estimate

Collect the acquisition date, sale date, purchase consideration, sale proceeds and documented transaction expenses before calculating a gain. For securities, broker contract notes and statements may help reconcile purchase lots and sale proceeds. For property, purchase and sale deeds plus eligible improvement and transfer-cost records may be relevant. Inherited or gifted assets can require tracing the previous owner's cost and acquisition date under applicable law. Do not assume that a simple difference between sale price and purchase price equals taxable capital gain. Tax rules can vary by asset, holding period and financial year, and exemptions may have conditions. Use this estimate as a preliminary working figure and verify the current tax treatment with official guidance or a qualified tax professional.

Avoid using a guessed flat tax rate

Do not assume that all gains are taxed at one universal percentage. The applicable treatment can depend on asset class, holding period, acquisition and transfer dates, residency, the relevant financial year and whether an exemption or special rule applies. Some assets may require separate treatment of each acquisition lot or a different cost-basis method. Keep the arithmetic estimate separate from the tax-rate decision. Before filing, reconcile the calculation with broker or sale statements and current official instructions. If records are incomplete or the transaction involves inheritance, gifts, corporate actions or property improvements, get qualified advice rather than treating the simple result as the final taxable figure.

Worked example with Indian rupees

If an asset is sold for ₹6,50,000 and its eligible cost basis is ₹4,00,000, the simple difference is ₹2,50,000 before eligible transaction costs, exemptions or other adjustments. Do not multiply that amount by a guessed tax rate without first identifying the asset, acquisition and sale dates, applicable law and any reliefs. Keep purchase records, sale statements and fee evidence so the calculation can be reconciled with tax reporting.
