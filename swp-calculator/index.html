What this calculator does

Estimate the remaining balance, total withdrawn and how long a corpus can fund a monthly withdrawal at an assumed return. Change the assumptions to compare outcomes. The result is an estimate based on the inputs you provide, not a promise or personalised financial recommendation.

How the calculation works

Each month the planned withdrawal is taken from the balance at the start of the month, and the remaining balance then earns the monthly equivalent of the assumed annual return (annual rate ÷ 12). The withdrawal is increased once a year by the step-up percentage. If the balance cannot cover a full withdrawal, the corpus is treated as exhausted. Taxes, exit loads, fund expenses and return volatility are not modelled.

How to use this calculator

For this calculator, check these inputs and units: Starting corpus; Monthly withdrawal; Assumed annual return; Planned withdrawal period; Annual increase in withdrawal; Total amount withdrawn; Investment growth earned. Keep monthly and annual figures separate, and change one input at a time when comparing scenarios.

Read the result in context

The output is the mathematical result of the assumptions entered, not a forecast of what a bank, employer, tax authority or investment provider will necessarily apply. Separate amounts you contribute or pay from interest, growth, tax or other calculated components where the tool provides them. A larger final amount is not always a better outcome: total interest, liquidity, affordability, risk and the time available can matter just as much as the headline number. Keep a note of your inputs when comparing two scenarios.

Compare more than one scenario

Run a baseline estimate first. Then create a conservative case and a second case with one important assumption changed, such as the rate, period, payment or contribution. Comparing scenarios is more informative than selecting a single result because it shows how sensitive the outcome is to the inputs. For long periods, even a small change in an assumed rate can have a large effect. For short periods, fees, timing and fixed charges can be relatively more important than a percentage-rate difference.

What this estimate does not include

This calculator does not automatically know current product terms, policy changes, eligibility conditions, fees, taxes, penalties, contribution ceilings or personal circumstances. Those items can change the real amount you receive or pay. For tax-related estimates, verify the applicable tax year, regime and official rules. For deposits, loans and retirement products, compare the result with the provider current documentation. If the result will affect a major financial decision, confirm the assumptions with a qualified professional or the relevant official source.

Example inputs

One illustrative set of inputs is: Starting corpus: 5000000; Monthly withdrawal: 30000; Assumed annual return: 8; Planned withdrawal period: 20; Annual increase in withdrawal: 5. Use your own figures for a more relevant estimate.

Frequently asked questions

What is a systematic withdrawal plan?

A systematic withdrawal plan, or SWP, is a standing instruction to redeem a fixed amount from an investment every month. The remaining money stays invested and keeps earning the assumed return.

Why does the corpus run out even though the return is positive?

Because each withdrawal is larger than the growth on the remaining balance in the later years, especially when the withdrawal steps up every year. The balance falls slowly at first and then faster.

Does this include tax on withdrawals?

No. Part of every withdrawal from a market-linked fund can be a taxable gain. Check the current capital-gains rules for your fund type before relying on the post-tax amount.

Important assumptions and limitations

This tool models the formula and assumptions described above. Actual outcomes can differ because of fees, taxes, eligibility rules, contribution timing, rate changes and individual circumstances. Check current official guidance or your provider's terms before making a financial decision.

How to read an SWP result without overestimating your corpus

A systematic withdrawal plan is a race between two numbers: the growth on the money that is left and the amount you take out. The result is most useful when you test the withdrawal against more than one return assumption instead of trusting a single rate. Start with a conservative return, then raise it and note how much the years of coverage change. Pay particular attention to the yearly increase in withdrawal. A fixed withdrawal loses buying power every year, while a stepped-up withdrawal protects it but drains the corpus faster. This model takes each withdrawal at the start of the month and ignores tax, exit loads and fund expenses, so real balances will usually be somewhat lower.

Why the withdrawal rate matters more than the return

Two plans with the same return can behave very differently if one withdraws 7% of the corpus in year one and the other withdraws 4%. A higher starting withdrawal leaves less money invested to earn the return, so the balance falls faster once markets are flat for a few years. Compare the first-year withdrawal with the starting corpus before you read the final balance. If the first-year figure is above roughly 6% of the corpus, the plan depends heavily on strong returns for many years.

Sequence risk and the limits of a smooth return

This calculator applies one steady return every year. Real markets move unevenly, and bad years early in the plan hurt far more than bad years at the end because withdrawals are taken from a smaller base. Treat the output as a planning range, keep an emergency fund outside the corpus, and revisit the withdrawal amount every year. Taxes on the gain portion of each withdrawal are not included and depend on the type of fund and the holding period.

Worked example with Indian rupees

With a starting corpus of ₹50,00,000, a first-year withdrawal of ₹30,000 a month that rises 5% every year and an assumed 8% return, the total withdrawn is about ₹1.07 crore and the corpus can pay the full withdrawal for about 18.6 years. It is exhausted before the planned 20 years. With no yearly increase, the same plan pays ₹72,00,000 over 20 years and still leaves about ₹68.5 lakh. Lowering the starting withdrawal to ₹25,000 with the 5% step-up leaves roughly ₹30.5 lakh after 20 years.
