How to Calculate Real Return After Inflation

A nominal return tells you how an amount changed in currency terms. A real return asks a harder and more useful question: how much did its purchasing power change after inflation?

Written and maintained by Wealth Arrays. Last reviewed: September 16, 2026. This guide explains the mathematics of inflation-adjusted returns; it does not forecast future investment performance.

What real return means

Suppose an investment grows by 10% while the general price level rises by 6%. Your account balance has increased by 10%, but the things you buy have also become more expensive. Real return measures the growth after adjusting for that change in purchasing power.

This distinction matters for long-term goals. A future balance that looks impressive in today's currency may represent much less spending power than the headline number suggests.

The exact real-return formula

The precise relationship is:

Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1

Both rates are expressed as decimals in the calculation. The formula compounds the two effects instead of simply subtracting one percentage from the other.

Worked example: 10% return and 6% inflation

With a nominal return of 10% and inflation of 6%, the calculation is (1.10 ÷ 1.06) − 1, which is approximately 3.77%. A quick approximation of 10% − 6% gives 4%, which is close for moderate rates but is not the exact result.

The gap becomes more noticeable when either rate is much larger. For precise planning, use the exact formula rather than the subtraction shortcut.

Real return can be negative

If inflation is higher than the nominal return, the real return can be below zero. For example, a 4% nominal return during 6% inflation produces an exact real return of about −1.89%. The account may still contain more currency units, but each unit buys less.

Return, inflation and purchasing power are different inputs

Do not mix the concepts. Nominal return describes the growth assumption for the asset or account. Inflation describes the change in the general price level. Real return is the mathematical result after comparing the two.

For a long-term projection, you can also calculate the purchasing power of a future amount by discounting it for the assumed inflation rate. That answers a different question from simply asking for the percentage real return.

Why a single inflation rate can be misleading

Headline inflation is an average measure. Your personal spending basket can behave differently. Housing, education, healthcare, food, transport and other categories can change at different rates. If a goal is specifically tied to one expense, its future cost may not track a general inflation assumption exactly.

Taxes and fees can reduce the real outcome

The basic real-return formula does not automatically account for taxes, platform charges, fund expenses, transaction costs or other deductions. If the return input is before costs and tax, the resulting real return is also before those effects. For a more realistic scenario, model the return after the costs that are known and relevant.

How to use an inflation-adjusted result

  1. Start with a clearly defined nominal return assumption.
  2. Choose an inflation assumption that is appropriate for the planning horizon.
  3. Calculate the exact real return.
  4. For a major goal, test several combinations rather than relying on one forecast.
  5. Keep taxes, fees and irregular cash flows separate unless the calculator explicitly models them.

Use the calculator as a scenario tool

The Inflation Calculator can help translate a future amount into today's purchasing-power terms. The important point is not to treat one rate as a prediction. A range of scenarios can show how sensitive a goal is to inflation.

A useful mental check

When someone says an investment “grew 100%,” ask over what period, after which costs, and during what inflation environment. Total growth alone does not tell you how much purchasing power was created.
