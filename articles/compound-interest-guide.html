Compound Interest Explained: Formula, Frequency, Inflation and Examples

Compound interest means previously credited interest becomes part of the balance that can earn future interest. The mathematics is simple, but the result depends heavily on the starting amount, rate, time and compounding frequency.

Written and maintained by Wealth Arrays. Last reviewed: September 14, 2026. This guide explains the math and does not predict a product's future return.

The standard formula

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). P is the starting principal, r is the annual rate expressed as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years. The formula assumes the stated rate remains constant for the entire period.

Worked example

Imagine ₹10,000 is modeled at a hypothetical 6% annual rate compounded monthly for 10 years. The calculation repeatedly adds the periodic interest to the balance. The result is larger than simple interest because later periods earn interest on both the original principal and earlier credited interest.

Why frequency changes the result

At the same nominal annual rate, monthly and annual compounding do not produce exactly the same ending value. More frequent compounding generally produces a slightly higher result when the quoted nominal rate is unchanged. Always check whether a bank or product quotes a nominal rate, effective annual rate or another convention before comparing numbers.

Time is the multiplier

Compounding needs time to become noticeable. In early years, the growth generated by the original principal can dominate. Later, the accumulated balance itself becomes a larger base. This is why extending a time horizon can matter more than making a small change to the starting amount.

Nominal growth is not purchasing-power growth

A balance can rise while its purchasing power grows more slowly. If inflation averages 5% and a hypothetical investment earns 6% nominally, the gap is much smaller than a simple “6% growth” headline suggests. Inflation is not automatically subtracted from the compound-interest formula; it is a separate assumption used to think about real value.

Common mistakes

  • Entering 6 instead of 0.06 when a formula expects a decimal.
  • Confusing annual rate with monthly rate.
  • Comparing products with different compounding conventions without checking their definitions.
  • Assuming a constant rate is a forecast of market performance.
  • Ignoring taxes, fees or withdrawals when those materially affect the real outcome.

When to use the calculator

Use compound-interest calculations to understand how assumptions interact and to compare scenarios. For investments with uncertain returns, treat the output as a mathematical illustration rather than a promise. For deposits or loans, use the product's stated compounding and payment rules when those details are available.
