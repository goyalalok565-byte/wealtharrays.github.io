BANKING GUIDE

Compound Interest Explained

Compound interest means interest can itself become part of the balance that earns future interest. That simple idea explains why long time horizons can produce surprisingly large differences.

The standard formula

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). P is the starting principal, r is the annual rate written as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years.

Why frequency matters

At the same nominal annual rate, monthly compounding generally produces a slightly different result from annual compounding because interest is added to the balance more often. The difference becomes more visible over longer periods or at higher rates.

Example

If 5,000 units were left invested for 10 years at a hypothetical 6% annual rate compounded monthly, the ending balance would be calculated from the formula above. The example is mathematical only; it does not predict what a bank, fund or market will pay.

Inflation is separate

A nominal balance can rise while purchasing power grows more slowly. To think about real growth, compare the assumed return with an appropriate inflation assumption and remember that actual inflation also changes over time.
