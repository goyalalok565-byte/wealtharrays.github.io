SIP Calculator Guide: Formula, Assumptions and How to Compare Scenarios

A systematic investment plan (SIP) is simply a recurring contribution made at regular intervals. A SIP calculator does not know the future. It applies a mathematical return assumption to a stream of contributions so you can see how different savings rates, time periods and assumptions change a projection.

Written and maintained by Wealth Arrays. Last reviewed: September 14, 2026. This guide explains the calculation model; it is not personalised investment advice.

How the SIP calculation works

For a monthly contribution, a common future-value model is FV = P × [((1+i)^n − 1) / i] × (1+i), where P is the monthly contribution, i is the monthly return assumption and n is the number of monthly contributions. The final multiplication by (1+i) represents contributions being treated as occurring at the beginning of each period in this model. If the rate is zero, the calculation simplifies to P × n.

What the numbers actually mean

Suppose you invest ₹5,000 per month for 15 years and test a hypothetical 10% annual return. Your own contributions are ₹9,00,000 over 180 months. The remaining amount in the projection is modeled growth. That distinction matters: the calculator is showing what a constant assumed rate would mathematically produce, not what a market is guaranteed to deliver.

Why the return assumption matters so much

Long periods amplify small differences in the assumed rate. That does not mean you should simply choose the highest rate. A better approach is to test a range of conservative, middle and optimistic scenarios and compare the required contribution under each. If a goal only works under an unusually high return assumption, the plan is fragile.

Monthly investing is not the same as a guaranteed monthly return

Real investments rarely grow in a straight line. Markets can rise, fall and remain flat for long periods. A calculator using a fixed annual rate smooths all of that volatility into one assumption. This is useful for planning mathematics, but it hides the path of returns. It also does not model every fund fee, tax treatment, exit load or product-specific rule unless those items are explicitly included.

Five checks before trusting a SIP projection

  1. Check the contribution. Make sure the monthly amount is realistic after essential expenses and emergency savings.
  2. Test more than one return. Never build a plan around a single optimistic number.
  3. Check inflation. A future ₹ amount is not directly comparable with today's purchasing power.
  4. Separate investment math from product choice. The calculator does not decide whether a particular fund or product is suitable.
  5. Stress-test the goal. Try a shorter period, lower return and higher contribution to see what breaks first.

Common SIP calculator mistakes

One common mistake is entering an annual rate as if it were a monthly rate. Another is comparing a pre-tax projection with an after-tax outcome. It is also easy to assume that increasing the return assumption by two percentage points is harmless. Over a long horizon it can materially change the projected value, which is exactly why scenario analysis is more useful than one headline number.

When a SIP calculator is useful

Use it to answer questions such as: “How much might I need to contribute each month to reach a target?” or “How sensitive is this goal to time and return assumptions?” Do not use it as evidence that a particular investment will earn a specific return.
