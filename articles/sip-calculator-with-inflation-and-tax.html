SIP Calculator with Inflation and Tax: How to Read a Realistic Projection

Written and maintained by Alok Goyal, founder and creator of Wealth Arrays. This guide is educational and not personalised financial advice.

A SIP calculator can answer a useful mathematical question: what could a series of regular contributions grow to under a stated return assumption? The difficult part starts when a future number is treated as if it already accounts for inflation, taxes, fees and the uncertainty of actual investment returns.

A more realistic SIP analysis separates those questions. First calculate the nominal projection. Then examine purchasing power after inflation. Finally consider the tax and cost assumptions that could reduce what is actually available to you. These layers should not be mixed into one unexplained percentage.

1. Start with the nominal SIP calculation

Record four basic inputs: monthly contribution, investment period, assumed annual return and contribution timing. If the contribution happens at the beginning of each period, the mathematical result differs from an otherwise identical end-of-period contribution.

For example, suppose a scenario uses ₹10,000 per month for 15 years. The total amount contributed is ₹18 lakh before considering any growth. A projected final value above ₹18 lakh is partly explained by the assumed investment return. Keeping the contribution total visible prevents the entire increase from being described as investment performance.

The result is a model, not a forecast of the actual path of a market. Actual returns can vary by year, and a constant-return assumption cannot reproduce volatility or the sequence in which returns occur.

2. Inflation answers a different question

Nominal value tells you how many currency units the projection contains in the future. Inflation asks what those future currency units may buy relative to today's prices.

If a retirement or education goal is stated in today's spending power, inflation should be modeled separately. A future target can be increased to reflect an assumed inflation rate, or a future corpus can be translated into an approximate present-value purchasing-power figure. The calculation depends on the period and the inflation assumption.

Do not simply subtract inflation from an investment return and call the result an exact real return. For a consistent annual model, the relationship is multiplicative:

Real return = (1 + nominal return) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1

This distinction matters when the two percentages are large or when a long horizon magnifies a small modeling difference.

3. Tax should be treated as an assumption, not a universal deduction

There is no single tax percentage that can be safely applied to every SIP projection. The tax treatment can depend on the investment product, holding period, transaction history, investor circumstances and the tax rules applicable at the time.

That is why a calculator should make clear whether its displayed result is before tax or after a particular illustrative tax assumption. A pre-tax projection is not necessarily an estimate of the amount you can withdraw and keep.

For planning, it is usually clearer to maintain two figures: the modeled investment value before tax and a separate after-tax scenario when the relevant tax rules and assumptions are known. This avoids hiding a jurisdiction-specific tax model inside a generic return calculation.

4. Fees can matter even when tax is unchanged

Investment costs reduce the amount that compounds. Depending on the product, these can include fund-level expenses, transaction charges, platform costs or other applicable fees. A small annual cost can have a larger cumulative effect over a long horizon because money that is spent on costs is also money that cannot compound.

Do not assume that a SIP calculator's return input automatically includes every possible cost. Check the calculator's methodology and the product documents before interpreting the result as a net outcome.

5. A better three-layer SIP scenario

  1. Base projection: calculate the corpus using the stated contribution, period and nominal return assumption.
  2. Purchasing-power view: apply a clearly stated inflation assumption to understand what the future amount may represent in today's terms.
  3. Net outcome view: consider relevant fees and a separate tax scenario when the product and applicable rules are known.

This structure makes it easier to see which assumption changes the result. It also prevents an impressive future-value number from being mistaken for a guaranteed spendable amount.

6. Stress-test the return assumption

A single return assumption can create false precision. Run a lower, central and higher planning scenario while keeping the contribution and period fixed. Then repeat the exercise with an inflation assumption that is different from the base case.

The purpose of a sensitivity test is not to predict the future. It is to identify how dependent the plan is on assumptions that the investor cannot fully control.

If a target only works under the highest return assumption, that dependency is important information. Likewise, if the target remains workable across several assumptions, that gives a different kind of planning information.

7. What a SIP calculator does not know

  • It does not know the future market return or the sequence of returns.
  • It cannot determine your personal tax liability without a relevant tax model and current facts.
  • It does not automatically know product-specific fees unless those costs are explicitly modeled.
  • It does not know whether a contribution schedule will actually be maintained.
  • It cannot decide whether a particular investment is appropriate for your circumstances.

8. Worked planning example

Imagine a household contributes ₹15,000 per month for 20 years. The nominal scenario might produce a large future corpus under an assumed annual return. The first check should be the contribution total: ₹36 lakh over 240 monthly contributions, before any growth.

Next, apply an inflation scenario to understand the purchasing power of that future corpus. Then examine whether the chosen investment has costs and whether tax could apply when gains are realised or money is withdrawn. The point is not to force one final number; it is to expose the assumptions behind it.

Frequently asked questions

Can a SIP calculator include inflation?

Yes, if the calculator explicitly models inflation. Inflation is best shown as a separate purchasing-power scenario rather than silently changing the investment return assumption.

Does a SIP calculator automatically account for tax?

Not necessarily. Unless a calculator states the tax model and assumptions, treat its displayed corpus as a mathematical projection rather than a personal after-tax result.

Should I subtract inflation from the SIP return?

For an exact annual real-return calculation, use the multiplicative relationship between nominal return and inflation rather than simple subtraction.

Why can two SIP calculators show different results?

They may use different contribution timing, compounding conventions, rounding, return-period assumptions or step-up rules. Compare methodology before deciding that one calculation is wrong.

Is the SIP calculator result guaranteed?

No. A constant-return projection is an assumption-based mathematical scenario. Actual investment returns can vary substantially over time.
