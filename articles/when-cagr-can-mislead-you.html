When CAGR Can Mislead You

CAGR, or compound annual growth rate, converts a starting value, ending value and holding period into one annualized growth rate. It is useful, but it deliberately smooths the path between the two values.

Written and maintained by Wealth Arrays. Last reviewed: September 14, 2026.

The formula

CAGR = (Ending value ÷ Starting value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1. If ₹10,000 becomes ₹12,100 over two years, the CAGR is 10%. It does not mean the investment earned exactly 10% in each year.

What CAGR is good at

CAGR gives a clean annualized comparison for a simple start-to-finish investment. It is often easier to compare than a raw total gain when holding periods differ.

What CAGR hides

CAGR does not show volatility, drawdowns or the order of returns. Two investments can have the same start value, end value and CAGR while taking very different paths.

Cash flows change the question

If money was added or withdrawn during the period, a simple CAGR can fail to describe the investor's actual experience. Cash-flow-aware measures such as IRR or XIRR answer a different question and may be more appropriate.

Historical versus assumed CAGR

A historical CAGR describes what happened between two known values. A projected CAGR is a mathematical assumption. Neither means the same rate is guaranteed to continue.

Use context

  • Check the holding period.
  • Check whether there were contributions or withdrawals.
  • Consider fees and taxes.
  • Look at volatility and drawdowns.
  • Identify whether the rate is historical, assumed or contractually specified.

CAGR smooths the path

CAGR answers a specific question: what constant annual growth rate would turn the starting value into the ending value over the selected period? That is useful for comparing endpoints, but it removes the path taken between them. Two investments can have the same starting and ending values while experiencing very different yearly gains and losses.

Why volatility matters

Consider one investment that grows steadily and another that rises sharply, falls sharply and then recovers. If both finish at the same value after five years, their CAGRs are the same even though the experience and risk were not. For investment analysis, CAGR should therefore be read alongside volatility, drawdowns, cash flows and the period being measured.

CAGR also depends on the endpoints

Changing the starting or ending date can materially change the result. A particularly strong market month at one endpoint or a weak month at the other can make the annualized number look unusually attractive or unattractive. When possible, compare multiple periods rather than selecting the dates that produce the most appealing percentage.

Use CAGR as one measure

CAGR is valuable for communicating long-term growth in a compact way. It is less useful when there are frequent deposits or withdrawals because those cash flows change the meaning of a simple start-to-end comparison. In those cases, a cash-flow-aware return measure can provide better context.
