EMI & Loan Payments Explained: Amortization, Interest and Total Cost

An EMI is a scheduled loan payment. Each installment can contain both interest and principal, so a monthly payment alone does not tell you the full cost of borrowing.

Written and maintained by Wealth Arrays. Last reviewed: September 14, 2026. This guide explains standard loan mathematics; actual lender terms can differ.

The standard EMI formula

For a standard amortizing loan with a fixed periodic rate, EMI = P × r × (1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1). P is principal, r is the periodic interest rate and n is the number of payments. If the rate is zero, payment is principal divided by the number of payments.

Why early payments can contain more interest

Interest is generally calculated from the outstanding balance. At the beginning, that balance is largest. As principal is repaid, the balance falls and the interest portion usually falls as well. The exact schedule depends on the lender's payment timing and contract.

Why a lower EMI can cost more

Extending a loan term usually spreads repayment across more periods. That can reduce the monthly payment while increasing total interest. Compare monthly payment, total repayment and total interest together instead of choosing by EMI alone.

Costs outside the basic formula

A basic calculator may not include processing fees, insurance, taxes, late charges, prepayment terms, floating-rate changes or other lender-specific costs. Those can change the real cost materially. The calculator is a baseline, not a binding lender quote.

How to compare loans properly

  1. Keep principal and repayment frequency consistent.
  2. Use the lender's quoted rate and understand how it is applied.
  3. Compare total interest and total repayment as well as EMI.
  4. Add known fees and other mandatory costs.
  5. Check whether the rate can reset and whether prepayment changes the economics.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing loans only by monthly payment.
  • Assuming a floating rate stays unchanged.
  • Ignoring fees because they are outside the EMI formula.
  • Rounding rates too early.
  • Treating a calculator result as the lender's final offer.
