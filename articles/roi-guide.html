ROI vs Annualized Return: Formula, Limits and Examples

Return on investment (ROI) is a useful first measurement, but a percentage without a time period can hide an important part of the story. A 20% gain in one year and a 20% gain over five years have the same simple ROI but very different growth rates.

Written and maintained by Wealth Arrays. Last reviewed: September 16, 2026. This guide explains return mathematics and does not predict investment performance.

What simple ROI measures

Simple ROI is:

ROI = (final value − initial cost) ÷ initial cost × 100

If ₹10,000 becomes ₹12,000, the total ROI is 20%. If the value falls to ₹9,000, the ROI is −10%. The measure is straightforward because it compares the total change with the original amount.

Why the holding period matters

ROI does not tell you how long the money was invested. Suppose Investment A gains 20% in one year while Investment B gains 20% over five years. Calling both “20% returns” is mathematically true as a total change, but it is not a like-for-like annual growth comparison.

For a single starting value and ending value, CAGR can normalize the growth into an annualized rate:

CAGR = (ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ years) − 1

CAGR is a smoothing calculation. It does not mean the investment actually earned the same percentage every year.

A worked comparison

Imagine ₹10,000 becomes ₹12,000. If that happens in one year, the annualized growth rate is 20%. If it happens over five years, the CAGR is only about 3.71% per year. The total ROI is unchanged at 20%, but the annualized interpretation is very different.

Why cash flows change the question

Simple ROI and CAGR become less informative when the investor adds or withdraws money during the period. A ₹10,000 account that receives several additional deposits cannot be described accurately by treating the final balance as if all of it had been invested on day one.

Cash-flow-aware methods such as IRR or XIRR incorporate the timing of contributions and withdrawals. They answer a different question: what rate is consistent with the observed cash flows and ending value? Choose the measure that matches the data rather than forcing every situation into a simple ROI formula.

Fees and taxes can change the real result

A headline ROI can be calculated before or after costs. Those are not the same result. Transaction costs, platform fees, fund expenses, taxes and other deductions can reduce the amount the investor actually keeps.

When comparing two scenarios, keep the cost treatment consistent. If the calculator does not model a particular fee or tax, do not interpret its output as an exact after-tax return.

Nominal return versus real return

ROI also says nothing about inflation by itself. A positive nominal return can coexist with a much smaller increase in purchasing power. If inflation is important to the decision, examine the real return after inflation separately.

Historical result versus future assumption

A historical ROI is calculated from values that already occurred. A projected ROI uses assumptions about a future value. The same formula can describe both, but the meaning is different. A projection is not evidence that the assumed return will occur.

Which measure should you use?

  • ROI: quick view of total gain or loss relative to the starting cost.
  • CAGR: annualized comparison when there is one starting value, one ending value and a known holding period.
  • IRR/XIRR: useful when deposits or withdrawals occur at different times.
  • Real return: useful when inflation and purchasing power are central to the question.

A better return-analysis workflow

  1. Write down the starting amount and ending amount.
  2. Record the exact holding period.
  3. List any contributions or withdrawals and their dates.
  4. Decide whether fees and taxes are included.
  5. Calculate total ROI.
  6. Use CAGR or a cash-flow-aware measure when the situation requires it.
  7. Consider inflation when the decision is about future purchasing power.

Why percentages should not be read in isolation

Two investments can show the same ROI but have different durations, volatility, cash-flow patterns and costs. A return percentage is a measurement, not a complete description of an investment experience. The surrounding assumptions determine what the number actually means.
