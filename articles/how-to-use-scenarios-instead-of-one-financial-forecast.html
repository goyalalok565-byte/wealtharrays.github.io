PLANNING · SCENARIO ANALYSIS

Why Financial Scenarios Are Better Than One Forecast

Written and maintained by Alok Goyal, founder and creator of Wealth Arrays. This guide is educational and not personalised financial advice.

A financial calculator can produce a precise number from precise inputs. That precision can be misleading when the inputs themselves are uncertain. A stronger planning method is to build several plausible scenarios and examine which assumptions change the result most.

Why one forecast creates false confidence

Suppose a calculator shows that a monthly investment could become ₹50 lakh after twenty years at an assumed return. The arithmetic may be perfectly correct. The problem is the assumption: future returns are not known in advance.

The same issue appears with inflation, salary growth, loan rates, retirement spending and freelance income. One number can hide uncertainty. Scenario analysis makes that uncertainty visible.

Build three scenarios

A practical starting point is three cases:

  • Conservative: less favorable assumptions that still remain plausible for the purpose of the exercise.
  • Central: the assumptions you currently consider reasonable.
  • Stress: a deliberately difficult case designed to show where the plan becomes uncomfortable.

The goal is not to predict which scenario will happen. The goal is to understand the range of outcomes and the assumptions driving it.

Change one important assumption at a time first

If you change return, contribution, inflation and time horizon simultaneously, you may not know what caused the result to move. Start by changing one variable. Then combine the important changes into a complete scenario.

For example, in a SIP projection, first compare 8%, 10% and 12% assumed annual returns while keeping the monthly contribution and time constant. Then test what happens if the contribution is lower. Finally build a combined conservative case.

The SIP Calculator is useful for this type of comparison because the result changes directly with the assumptions you enter.

Use scenarios for debt too

Debt planning is not only about the scheduled EMI. If the interest rate is variable, create a higher-rate case. If you expect to make extra payments, create a scheduled-only case and a realistic extra-payment case.

The purpose is to find out whether the plan depends on everything going perfectly. If a loan is comfortable only when income remains high and rates remain low, that dependency should be visible before the commitment is made.

Stress-test inflation and retirement

Retirement plans are especially sensitive because the horizon is long. A small difference in annual inflation or investment return can compound for decades. Instead of asking for one “correct” retirement corpus, calculate several combinations.

For example, combine a moderate spending assumption with a higher-inflation case, then combine it with a lower-return case. You can also extend the retirement period. The Retirement Calculator and Inflation Calculator can be used as separate pieces of this analysis.

Focus on sensitivity, not the prettiest result

Sometimes one input dominates the outcome. If a small change in the assumed return produces a huge change in the projected corpus, return is a high-sensitivity assumption. If changing the contribution by a small amount has a larger effect, contribution is more important.

That information is valuable because it tells you where to spend your planning effort. You may have much more control over your contribution than over market returns. A scenario analysis can therefore shift attention from variables you merely hope for to variables you can actually influence.

Do not confuse a scenario with a probability

A conservative scenario is not necessarily “20% likely,” and a stress case is not necessarily the worst event that could happen. Unless you have a defensible statistical model, do not attach invented probabilities to calculator scenarios.

Use scenario analysis as a decision tool: “What happens if this assumption is different?” That is a safer question than “What will definitely happen?”

Keep the assumptions written down

When you revisit a calculation six months later, you may not remember why you chose a particular rate or time period. Record the date, inputs and reason for the assumptions. This makes the exercise auditable and lets you see whether the plan changed because your circumstances changed or because the original assumptions were unrealistic.

A practical scenario worksheet

  1. Write down the decision being tested.
  2. List the variables that materially affect the result.
  3. Create conservative, central and stress values.
  4. Change one variable at a time to identify sensitivity.
  5. Combine the important changes into complete scenarios.
  6. Record the date and assumptions.
  7. Decide which variables you can control and which you cannot.
  8. Review the scenario when the underlying circumstances change.

Where calculators fit

Calculators are excellent arithmetic engines. They are less useful when treated as crystal balls. Use them to make the consequences of assumptions explicit, then use the result to ask better questions.

For example, a CAGR result can show the annualized rate implied by a historical start and end value, while an ROI result can show the simple percentage change. A loan calculator can show how tenure affects payment and total interest. An inflation calculator can translate today's amount into a future-price scenario. Each tool answers a specific mathematical question.

Bottom line

Good financial planning does not require pretending that uncertain inputs are certain. Build several scenarios, identify the assumptions that matter most and focus on the variables you can control. A range of transparent calculations is usually more informative than one highly precise number based on a fragile assumption.
