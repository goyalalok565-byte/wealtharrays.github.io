What this calculator does

Compare loan-interest savings with investing the same extra cash for the remaining loan period. It keeps the assumptions visible so you can change one input at a time and understand what drives the result.

How the calculation works

Compare the interest avoided by reducing principal with the future value of the alternative investment.

Inputs

The calculator uses outstanding balance, loan rate, remaining term, prepayment amount and assumed investment return. Change the assumptions and click Calculate result to compare scenarios.

Limitations and methodology

Wealth Arrays performs deterministic browser-side calculations. It does not automatically know current market rates, lender policy, tax law, fees or personal eligibility. Verify current figures with the relevant official source or provider before making a material decision.

Frequently asked questions

Is prepaying a guaranteed return?

Interest avoided is more predictable than an investment return, but lender terms matter.

Does this prove SIP is better?

No. The result is sensitive to the assumed investment return, taxes and fees.

Should I keep an emergency fund first?

Do not use cash for prepayment if it leaves essential liquidity uncovered.

Compare loan prepayment with investing extra cash

This comparison weighs a relatively certain reduction in future loan interest against an uncertain investment outcome. Prepayment can reduce principal and interest according to the lender's amortisation and prepayment rules. An SIP projection depends on an assumed return and market risk. The two outcomes are not directly equivalent unless taxes, fees, timing, liquidity and risk are considered consistently. Use the tool to test scenarios, not to promise that investing will outperform debt repayment.

What the loan side represents

The interest saved from prepayment depends on outstanding principal, remaining tenure, interest-rate resets and the lender's allocation of extra payments. Confirm whether prepayment reduces EMI, shortens tenure or can be directed by you. Keep an emergency reserve before using all available cash to reduce a loan, because money paid into the loan may not be easy to access again.

What the investment side leaves uncertain

A projected SIP or investment return is an assumption rather than a contractual rate. Market returns can be negative over shorter periods and taxes, expense ratios and transaction costs affect net outcomes. Compare after-tax values over the same horizon and include risk tolerance and liquidity needs. A lower expected return may still be acceptable if flexibility is important; a higher projected return is not a guarantee.

Compare net outcomes and preserve liquidity

A fair comparison uses the same starting cash amount, time horizon and tax basis for both choices. On the loan side, confirm the outstanding principal, remaining tenure, prepayment fees and whether the lender reduces EMI or term after an extra payment. On the investment side, use net-of-cost return assumptions and include a weak or negative scenario. Interest avoided through prepayment may be more predictable than market returns, but prepayment reduces liquid cash and can be hard to reverse. Keep an emergency fund and account for any tax effects before deciding. The higher projected number should not automatically win if it requires taking risk you cannot afford or leaves too little accessible savings.

Account for taxes, fees and the value of flexibility

An investment projection should be compared after expense ratios, transaction costs and relevant taxes, not just by its assumed gross return. On the loan side, confirm whether the lender charges a prepayment fee and how the extra amount changes the amortisation schedule. Money used to prepay may reduce interest but become unavailable for an emergency; money invested may remain liquid but can fall in value when you need it. Consider the time horizon, risk tolerance and whether you have enough accessible savings before making a one-way decision. A scenario calculator can clarify trade-offs but cannot tell you which option is best for every household.

Worked example with Indian rupees

Suppose you have ₹1,00,000 available and a loan charging 9% a year. Applying that amount to principal reduces the first year's interest by roughly ₹9,000 if the full amount would otherwise remain outstanding for that year; the exact schedule saving depends on payment dates and the lender's recalculation. Investing the same amount at an assumed 10% would model to ₹1,10,000 after one year before fees and taxes, but that return is not guaranteed. Compare downside scenarios and preserve an emergency reserve.
