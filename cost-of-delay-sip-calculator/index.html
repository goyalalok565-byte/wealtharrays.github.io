What this calculator does

Compare starting a monthly SIP now with starting later for the same end date, and see the extra monthly amount needed to catch up. Change the assumptions to compare outcomes. The result is an estimate based on the inputs you provide, not a promise or personalised financial recommendation.

How the calculation works

SIP future value = monthly amount × [((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i] × (1 + i), where i is the annual rate ÷ 12 and n is the number of monthly instalments. Starting now uses all the months to the goal date. Starting later uses fewer months, ending on the same date. The monthly amount needed to catch up equals the start-now value divided by the delayed future-value factor.

How to use this calculator

For this calculator, check these inputs and units: Monthly SIP amount; Expected annual return; Years until the goal; Years you delay the start; Value if you start now; Value if you start later; Cost of the delay; Monthly SIP needed to catch up after the delay. Keep monthly and annual figures separate, and change one input at a time when comparing scenarios.

Read the result in context

The output is the mathematical result of the assumptions entered, not a forecast of what a bank, employer, tax authority or investment provider will necessarily apply. Separate amounts you contribute or pay from interest, growth, tax or other calculated components where the tool provides them. A larger final amount is not always a better outcome: total interest, liquidity, affordability, risk and the time available can matter just as much as the headline number. Keep a note of your inputs when comparing two scenarios.

Compare more than one scenario

Run a baseline estimate first. Then create a conservative case and a second case with one important assumption changed, such as the rate, period, payment or contribution. Comparing scenarios is more informative than selecting a single result because it shows how sensitive the outcome is to the inputs. For long periods, even a small change in an assumed rate can have a large effect. For short periods, fees, timing and fixed charges can be relatively more important than a percentage-rate difference.

What this estimate does not include

This calculator does not automatically know current product terms, policy changes, eligibility conditions, fees, taxes, penalties, contribution ceilings or personal circumstances. Those items can change the real amount you receive or pay. For tax-related estimates, verify the applicable tax year, regime and official rules. For deposits, loans and retirement products, compare the result with the provider current documentation. If the result will affect a major financial decision, confirm the assumptions with a qualified professional or the relevant official source.

Example inputs

One illustrative set of inputs is: Monthly SIP amount: 10000; Expected annual return: 12; Years until the goal: 25; Years you delay the start: 5. Use your own figures for a more relevant estimate.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the cost of delay larger than the contributions missed?

The early instalments have the longest time to compound. Missing the first years removes the money that would have grown the most, so the loss is much larger than the amount you skipped.

Can a larger SIP make up for starting late?

Yes, but it takes a higher monthly amount. The calculator shows the extra monthly investment that would reach the same end value from the delayed start.

Are the returns guaranteed?

No. The rate is an assumption used to show the effect of time. Market-linked returns vary year to year and can be negative.

Important assumptions and limitations

This tool models the formula and assumptions described above. Actual outcomes can differ because of fees, taxes, eligibility rules, contribution timing, rate changes and individual circumstances. Check current official guidance or your provider's terms before making a financial decision.

Why starting a SIP late costs more than the months you skip

The cost of delay is the difference between the corpus you would have if you started now and the corpus you would have if you started later but still stopped investing on the same goal date. The early instalments have the longest time to compound, so missing them removes the part of the investment that would have grown the most. This calculator also shows the larger monthly amount that would be needed from the later start to reach the same end value. Test it with your own goal date and a conservative return, because the rate is an assumption and real market returns vary from year to year.

The delay cost grows faster than the delay

Five years of delay cost about ₹89.8 lakh in the example, but ten years cost about ₹1.39 crore, and the required catch-up amount nearly doubles. That is because the missed months are the early ones that compound the longest. If you can only start with a smaller amount today, starting now and raising the SIP later is usually better than waiting for a larger amount.

Match the goal date to a real need

The calculator ends on a fixed goal date such as a child's education or retirement. Use a date that matches a real need and a return that you would be comfortable defending in a poor market. If the return you assume is high, the cost of delay looks larger, so run a lower return as well and compare the two results.

Worked example with Indian rupees

A monthly SIP of ₹10,000 at an assumed 12% return for 25 years grows to about ₹1.90 crore. Starting 5 years late for the same end date gives about ₹99.9 lakh, so the cost of the delay is about ₹89.8 lakh. To reach ₹1.90 crore from the delayed start you would need about ₹18,993 a month. A 10-year delay raises the cost to about ₹1.39 crore and the catch-up SIP to about ₹37,609. At a 10% return, the 5-year delay costs about ₹57.2 lakh.
